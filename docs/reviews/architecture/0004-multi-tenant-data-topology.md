## Purpose

Assess whether the hybrid topology is appropriate for the stated scale, workload skew, tenant isolation, migration, recovery, residency, availability, and cost drivers, and whether the ADR is sufficiently defined to advance into detailed design.

## Architecture Summary

The ADR selects a hybrid topology: shared-schema tenant shards or pools for standard tenants, with dedicated shards or databases for exceptional workload, regulatory, or contractual needs. An authoritative tenant directory resolves placement. Tenant context must be validated and propagated across application and data paths. Tenant migration is incremental, with a point after which recovery must use forward reconciliation rather than unsafe routing rollback. Tenant-scoped restore, deletion reconciliation, regional placement, and legacy-backup import are identified as required capabilities.

## Architectural Drivers

- Growth from approximately 100,000 tenants to 250,000–300,000.
- Highly skewed workloads, including largest tenants peaking at 500–1,000 TPS and aggregate bursts near 30,000 TPS.
- Interactive latency and tiered availability targets.
- Tenant isolation across application, data, recovery, and operational paths.
- Independent tenant recovery, with prototype RPO/RTO targets of 15 minutes/four hours.
- EU/EEA residency for primary, replica, backup, and recovery data.
- Incremental tenant migration with a five-minute final write-quiesce target.
- Reduced infrastructure cost and operational effort without unacceptable failure blast radius or noisy-neighbor impact.

## What Looks Sound

- The hybrid sharded-default topology addresses documented workload skew and exceptional regulatory, contractual, and workload needs without requiring dedicated databases for every tenant.
- The ADR compares credible alternatives and explains why a global shared database and schema-per-tenant are not strategic defaults.
- The tenant directory is placement metadata, not a store for tenant business state.
- Tenant identity must be validated; loss of tenant context fails closed.
- Application filtering alone is explicitly insufficient, and defense in depth is required for shared-schema isolation.
- Residency requirements include replicas, backups, and recovery copies.
- The migration point of no return rejects unsafe post-write routing rollback.
- The ADR distinguishes a justified architecture direction from unproven production targets and avoids prescribing technologies without evidence.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** The tenant directory is critical routing infrastructure, but its availability objective and behavior under outage, stale cache, or concurrent placement change remain unspecified.  
**Why it matters:** Directory unavailability can impair the data plane; stale placement can route operations to an obsolete location or undermine tenant isolation during movement.  
**Recommendation:** Define availability and recovery objectives, routing-version semantics, cache lifetime and invalidation, behavior during directory outage, and safe rejection of stale routes.

### Finding 2

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Shards are described as bounded failure domains, but the ADR does not set capacity limits or the maximum tenant population/workload exposed to one shard failure.  
**Why it matters:** Without measurable limits, shard overload or failure may affect more tenants than intended, and the scaling/isolation benefits cannot be objectively sized or tested.  
**Recommendation:** Establish measurable capacity and blast-radius limits for storage, throughput, connections, and recovery; validate them under representative load and failure scenarios.

### Finding 3

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Tenant-level recovery and deletion safety have a plausible workflow but no demonstrated isolation or measured recovery evidence.  
**Why it matters:** A shared-shard restore can affect neighboring tenants, miss prototype RPO/RTO targets, reintroduce deleted data, or violate residency requirements.  
**Recommendation:** Exercise tenant restore for shared and dedicated placements, including deletion-state reconciliation and residency enforcement; record measured RPO/RTO and prove neighboring tenant data remains isolated.

### Finding 4

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** The migration policy defines quiescing and a point of no return, but not write fencing or the authoritative-writer transition around cutover.  
**Why it matters:** In-flight requests, workers, or stale routes may continue writing to the legacy store after target activation, causing divergent state or loss of acknowledged writes.  
**Recommendation:** Define draining/fencing, route-generation changes, rejection of old writers, and the conditions that guarantee the target is the sole writer before it accepts writes.

### Finding 5

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Online copy/catch-up and forward reconciliation lack a consistency contract for ordering, checkpoints, duplicate handling, retries, and pre-cutover validation.  
**Why it matters:** A correctly fenced cutover cannot repair missing, duplicated, or out-of-order changes accumulated during copy and catch-up.  
**Recommendation:** Define change-capture ordering and checkpoint semantics, idempotency and duplicate handling, reconciliation rules, and cutover validation criteria; test partial failures and retries.

### Finding 6

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Workload-based promotion and shard sizing cannot be validated because placement thresholds and the performance acceptance workload are not specified.  
**Why it matters:** Aggregate TPS and latency targets do not define transaction mix, burst duration, concurrency, tenant distribution, or when a tenant must be throttled or promoted.  
**Recommendation:** Define a repeatable workload profile and measurable promotion/demotion criteria before production sizing or automated placement; test aggregate load and hot-tenant interference.

### Finding 7

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** The ADR requires defense in depth for shared-schema isolation but defers enforcement mechanisms and administrative access boundaries.  
**Why it matters:** Tenant-aware query abstractions without enforceable controls and verification leave cross-tenant access dependent on application correctness.  
**Recommendation:** Select and verify database, data-access, authorization, and administrative controls before production implementation; require automated negative tests proving cross-tenant reads and writes are denied.

### Finding 8

**Severity:** MINOR  
**Finding:** Legacy backup compatibility is transitional, but supported versions, validation/import acceptance criteria, and retirement conditions remain undecided.  
**Why it matters:** An unbounded compatibility path can become a permanent maintenance and security obligation or fail to support the intended migration population.  
**Recommendation:** Define the supported-version window, integrity checks, import acceptance criteria, and retirement trigger before migration rollout.

### Finding 9

**Severity:** MINOR  
**Finding:** Tenant-aware telemetry is required, but controls for tenant identifiers in logs, metrics, and traces—including access, retention, and metric cardinality—are not defined.  
**Why it matters:** Tenant diagnostics may expose sensitive operational context, and high-cardinality dimensions can undermine observability cost and usability.  
**Recommendation:** Define authorized use, redaction, retention, and cardinality rules; prefer controlled correlation or support workflows over unrestricted metric dimensions.

## Alternatives Worth Considering

The hybrid model, with a bounded shard/pool default and dedicated exceptions, remains a credible balance for the stated drivers. The ADR rejects a global shared database and schema-per-tenant for relevant recovery, blast-radius, and scale reasons. No materially simpler topology is evident that meets the same combination of workload isolation, recovery, residency, and tenant-count goals. Retain the simpler sharded-pool default unless evidence justifies additional placement classes.

## Required Decisions

- Directory availability, cache/version consistency, and stale-routing behavior.
- Shard capacity and acceptable failure/overload blast radius.
- Tenant promotion/demotion thresholds and a repeatable workload acceptance profile.
- Shared-schema isolation enforcement and privileged administrative access.
- Copy/catch-up ordering, checkpoints, retries, idempotency, reconciliation, and cutover validation.
- Cutover fencing, in-flight operation handling, and the single-writer guarantee.
- Recovery proof for tenant isolation, deletion replay, residency, RPO, and RTO.
- Supported legacy backup versions, validation criteria, and retirement conditions.
- Tenant identifier access, retention, and telemetry cardinality controls.

## Conditions

1. Keep the ADR proposed until the authorized decision owner accepts the topology.
2. Resolve directory failure/stale-route semantics, shared-schema isolation controls, and migration fencing before production migration or implementation.
3. Define shard capacity, workload profiles, and placement thresholds; validate throughput, latency, noisy-neighbor behavior, and failure blast radius.
4. Define and test the online-copy/catch-up consistency contract, including retry and partial-failure behavior, before production migration.
5. Demonstrate tenant-level recovery for shared and dedicated placements, including deletion safety, residency, and measured RPO/RTO, before production adoption.
6. Define the legacy-backup support window and tenant telemetry privacy/cardinality controls before operational rollout.
7. Treat cost, provisioning, schema rollout, and manual-intervention targets as hypotheses until measured against current baselines.

APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS

The hybrid sharded-default direction is justified by the requirements and remains appropriate to advance into focused detailed design. This approval is limited to the architecture direction; it is not approval for production implementation or migration. Resolve the significant findings and conditions at the appropriate design and production-readiness gates.

## Remaining Risks

- Directory outage or stale placement may impair availability or route work incorrectly.
- Unbounded shard sizing may defeat intended failure containment.
- Unproven migration consistency or unfenced cutover may leave divergent authoritative writes.
- Recovery may miss RPO/RTO targets, affect neighboring tenants, resurrect deleted data, or breach residency.
- Unverified isolation controls may permit cross-tenant access.
- Cost and operational-effort targets remain unproven until measured.

## Next Engineering Activity

Continue detailed design for the tenant directory/control plane, shared-shard isolation, migration copy/catch-up and fencing/reconciliation, and tenant-level recovery. Define a repeatable workload and failure-test profile, then update the ADR with decisions and evidence before production implementation.
