## Purpose

Reconfirm whether the hybrid topology remains appropriate for the documented requirements and whether the ADR is sufficiently defined to advance into detailed design.

## Architecture Summary

The ADR selects a hybrid topology: shared-schema tenant shards or pools for standard tenants, with dedicated shards or databases for exceptional workload, regulatory, or contractual needs. An authoritative tenant directory resolves placement. Tenant context must be validated and propagated across application and data paths. Tenant migration is incremental, with a point after which recovery must use forward reconciliation rather than unsafe routing rollback. Tenant-scoped restore, deletion reconciliation, regional placement, and legacy-backup import are identified as required capabilities.

## Architectural Drivers

- Growth from approximately 100,000 tenants to 250,000–300,000.
- Highly skewed workloads, including largest tenants peaking at 500–1,000 TPS and aggregate bursts near 30,000 TPS.
- Interactive latency and tiered availability targets.
- Tenant isolation across application, data, recovery, and operational paths.
- Independent tenant recovery, with prototype RPO/RTO targets of 15 minutes/four hours.
- EU/EEA residency for primary, replica, backup, and recovery data.
- Incremental tenant migration with a five-minute final write-quiesce target.
- Reduced infrastructure cost and operational effort without unacceptable failure blast radius or noisy-neighbor impact.

## What Looks Sound

- The hybrid sharded-default topology addresses workload skew and exceptional regulatory, contractual, and workload needs without requiring dedicated databases for every tenant.
- The ADR evaluates credible alternatives and explains why a global shared database and schema-per-tenant are not strategic defaults.
- The tenant directory is placement metadata rather than tenant business state.
- Tenant identity must be validated; loss of tenant context fails closed.
- Application filtering alone is explicitly insufficient; defense in depth is required for shared-schema isolation.
- Residency covers replicas, backups, and recovery copies.
- The migration point of no return rejects unsafe post-write routing rollback.
- The ADR distinguishes architecture direction from unproven production targets.

## Findings

The target content is unchanged from review #0004; its findings remain applicable.

### Finding 1

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Tenant-directory availability objectives and behavior under outage, stale cache, or concurrent placement change are unspecified.  
**Why it matters:** Outage can impair the data plane; stale placement can route operations incorrectly or undermine tenant isolation.  
**Recommendation:** Define availability and recovery objectives, routing-version semantics, cache lifetime/invalidation, outage behavior, and safe stale-route rejection.

### Finding 2

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Shard capacity limits and the maximum tenant population/workload exposed to a shard failure are unspecified.  
**Why it matters:** Without measurable limits, overload or failure may affect more tenants than intended and claimed failure containment cannot be validated.  
**Recommendation:** Establish measurable storage, throughput, connection, recovery, and blast-radius limits; validate them under representative load and failure scenarios.

### Finding 3

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Tenant-level recovery and deletion safety have a proposed workflow but no measured proof.  
**Why it matters:** Shared-shard restore may affect neighboring tenants, miss prototype RPO/RTO targets, resurrect deleted data, or violate residency requirements.  
**Recommendation:** Test recovery for shared and dedicated placements, deletion reconciliation, and residency; record measured RPO/RTO and demonstrate tenant isolation.

### Finding 4

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Migration quiescing and point-of-no-return semantics are stated, but write fencing and the authoritative-writer transition around cutover are not defined.  
**Why it matters:** In-flight requests, workers, or stale routes may continue writing to the legacy store after target activation.  
**Recommendation:** Define draining/fencing, route-generation changes, rejection of old writers, and the conditions ensuring the target is the sole writer before accepting writes.

### Finding 5

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Online copy/catch-up and forward reconciliation lack a consistency contract for ordering, checkpoints, duplicate handling, retries, and pre-cutover validation.  
**Why it matters:** Missing, duplicated, or out-of-order changes can leave the target inconsistent even if final cutover is fenced.  
**Recommendation:** Define change-capture ordering/checkpoints, idempotency, duplicate handling, reconciliation, and cutover validation; test partial failures and retries.

### Finding 6

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Placement thresholds and a repeatable performance acceptance workload are not defined.  
**Why it matters:** Aggregate TPS and latency targets do not specify transaction mix, burst duration, concurrency, tenant distribution, or promotion/throttling thresholds.  
**Recommendation:** Define a repeatable workload and measurable promotion/demotion criteria before production sizing or automated placement; test aggregate load and hot-tenant interference.

### Finding 7

**Severity:** SIGNIFICANT  
**Finding:** Shared-schema isolation enforcement and privileged administrative access boundaries are deferred.  
**Why it matters:** Tenant-aware query abstractions without enforceable controls and verification leave cross-tenant access dependent on application correctness.  
**Recommendation:** Select and verify database, data-access, authorization, and administrative controls before production; require negative tests for cross-tenant reads and writes.

### Finding 8

**Severity:** MINOR  
**Finding:** Supported legacy backup versions, import acceptance criteria, and retirement conditions are undecided.  
**Why it matters:** Unbounded compatibility can become a permanent maintenance/security obligation or fail to support the migration population.  
**Recommendation:** Define the support window, integrity checks, import criteria, and retirement trigger before migration rollout.

### Finding 9

**Severity:** MINOR  
**Finding:** Controls for tenant identifiers in logs, metrics, and traces—including access, retention, and metric cardinality—are undefined.  
**Why it matters:** Tenant diagnostics may expose sensitive operational context, and high-cardinality dimensions can undermine observability cost and usability.  
**Recommendation:** Define authorized use, redaction, retention, and cardinality rules; prefer controlled correlation or support workflows over unrestricted metric dimensions.

## Alternatives Worth Considering

The hybrid model, with a bounded shard/pool default and dedicated exceptions, remains a credible balance for the stated drivers. The ADR rejects a global shared database and schema-per-tenant for relevant recovery, blast-radius, and scale reasons. No materially simpler topology is evident that meets the same combination of workload isolation, recovery, residency, and tenant-count goals.

## Required Decisions

- Directory availability, cache/version consistency, and stale-routing behavior.
- Shard capacity and acceptable failure/overload blast radius.
- Tenant promotion/demotion thresholds and repeatable workload profile.
- Shared-schema isolation enforcement and privileged administrative access.
- Copy/catch-up ordering, checkpoints, retry/idempotency, reconciliation, and cutover validation.
- Cutover fencing and single-writer guarantee.
- Recovery evidence for tenant isolation, deletion replay, residency, RPO, and RTO.
- Legacy backup support scope and retirement conditions.
- Tenant telemetry access, retention, and cardinality controls.

## Conditions

1. Keep the ADR proposed until accepted by the authorized decision owner.
2. Resolve directory failure/stale-route behavior, shared-schema isolation controls, and migration fencing before production implementation or migration.
3. Define shard capacity, workload profiles, and placement thresholds; validate throughput, latency, noisy-neighbor behavior, and blast radius.
4. Define and test the online-copy/catch-up consistency contract, including retries and partial failures.
5. Demonstrate tenant-level recovery for shared and dedicated placements, including deletion safety, residency, and measured RPO/RTO.
6. Define the legacy-backup support window and tenant telemetry privacy/cardinality controls before operational rollout.
7. Validate cost, provisioning, schema rollout, and manual-intervention targets against current baselines.

## Remaining Risks

- Directory outage or stale placement may impair availability or route work incorrectly.
- Unbounded shard sizing may defeat intended failure containment.
- Unproven migration consistency or unfenced cutover may leave divergent authoritative writes.
- Recovery may miss objectives, affect neighboring tenants, resurrect deleted data, or breach residency.
- Unverified isolation controls may permit cross-tenant access.
- Cost and operational-effort targets remain unproven until measured.
